# Quantarune: Project Automaton

From February 2026 to June 2026, I competed in Intrinsic’s AI for Industry Challenge, finishing it with Rucha Agashe as Team Quantarune. At the time, I wanted to pivot from building with agentic systems and post-training language models to Physical AI, because I believed robotics is the future. This was my first proper robotics project. I was most interested in reinforcement learning and simulations, and hoped that the competition would allow me to apply RL for once. I even bought an RTX 5090 workstation for it.

# What is AIC

<img
  src="/content/portfolio/robotics/projects/project-automaton/images/intrinsic-workcell.png"
  alt="Intrinsic's AIC workcell: a UR5e arm above a fiber-optic task board"
  style={{width: "100%", borderRadius: "8px", marginBottom: "0.5rem"}}
/>

*Intrinsic's AI for Industry Challenge Gazebo workbench: a UR5e arm and the fiber-optic task board.*

Intrinsic’s AI for Industry Challenge is a competition run by Alphabet’s Intrinsic with NVIDIA and Foxconn. Teams are asked to create a robot policy that gets a Universal Robots UR5e robotic arm to insert a fiber-optic cable into a server port across two connector types (SC & SFP), inside Gazebo. There was a qualification round and a final round; we didn’t get past qualification, finishing 49th out of 196 teams. The competition scored every submission with Intrinsic’s custom-created evaluation-engine (the AIC engine) inside Gazebo, across three held-out trials. This is a very difficult challenge, because cable insertion is a hard task in robotics: the contact and friction dynamics of cable ports are incredibly difficult to model, and tends to be where policies fail.

So, we recorded the trajectories from `CheatCode.py` across 100+ domain randomised episodes per connector type, and trained ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers) on these trajectories.

At this point, the first part, getting the TCP from the dock to just on top of the correct port, worked. The bottleneck was the last few millimeters, where the TCP had to actually insert into the port, and handle contact dynamics. This, final-stage cable insertion, was the kind of task where I had pinned my hopes and anticipation on reinforcement learning in robotic simulation, as it’s the whole reason I entered this competition—because of my love and admiration for the power of reinforcement learning’s ceiling. 

It didn’t work. Perhaps it was because I lacked the necessary skills because this was my first proper reinforcement learning project, however this made me realise, first-hand, the industry consensus about how it’s incredibly hard to make RL work, especially for contact-rich manipulation. The search space was ginormous and the reward signal is very sparse, and after weeks without progress we had also dropped this idea.

I also experimented with adding perception, with CNOS for segmentation and MegaPose for 6D pose estimation. The idea was that this would improve policy performance in training, as the policy can now tell apart the ports, from the task board, from the white table. Ultimately, we dropped the computer vision models due to competition compute-constraints. Because the held-out eval submissions were evaluated using a single NVIDIA L4 GPU, and adding two ViTs on top of our ACT policy was too much for the single L4 GPU to handle. The perception-overhead would’ve increased inference latency so much so that our control frequency would be decreased to the point where the policy could no longer handle fine-insertion, inside Gazebo, let alone the real world!

In the end, with the qualification deadline nearing, we went boring, and boring got us closer than any learned policies did. We replaced the single end-to-end ACT policy with a four-state machine, so each constrained state could use whatever worked:

- **APPROACH**: ACT drives the TCP from the dock towards the port. Advances to the next state once the model’s target has settled to within 25 mm.
- **ALIGN**: still ACT, but damped. Instead of naively moving the TCP straight to each new target the ACT model produces, the arm moves only a quarter of the way towards it per step, so a noisy prediction can’t throw the tip off. The stage ends when lateral XY-error is \<15mm and angular error \<0.12 rad, and both errors hold for \>0.75 seconds. At this point, the policy reads the direction the ACT model has been pushing in and locks it as the insert axis. 
- **GUARDED_INSERT**: ACT is switched off. The arm steps along the fixed axis about 5mm per quarter-second, decelerating if the force-torque sensor climbs from 12N to 19N. A supervisor also clamps every step (\<1mm of lateral drift, and \<0.01 radian of rotation) and aborts on four triggers:
  - Force-torque sensor >18N
  - 1.5 seconds with no progress (indicating TCP getting stuck on port)
  - 6 stalled GUARDED_INSERT attempts
  - Lateral oscillations
- **RETRY_OR_FINISH**: if TCP was aborted during GUARDED_INSERT, we return to ALIGN to try once more, else task success.

The ACT models behind APPROACH and ALIGN were trained on our custom-curated recorded trajectories. We made CheatCode (the scripted reference policy from Intrinsic) run under perturbations, and used our teleoperated insertions, so the model had seen clean approaches, near-misses and recoveries rather than only best case scenarios. The insertion portion of our policy (GUARDED_INSERT) had no learned components, it relies only on analytically controlled force-scaled stepping. This consequently was our only policy which reliably made contact without slamming into the ports.

By then it was mid-June, and I was in the middle of my first-year Computing final exams at Imperial, so the last few weeks had far fewer hours for which I’ve allocated for this competition than I ideally should have. We only ended up finishing the FSM-ACT policy after my finals, and days before the qualification deadline and submitted it. 

On Team Quantarune’s local benchmark, our FSM-ACT policy scored 141 points. Decent, not ideal (SFP inserted, SC mostly didn’t), but it would’ve been enough for us to qualify for the finals. However, on the hidden competition evals, our FSM-ACT policy only scored 89 points.

We worked out why too late. We misread the competition documentations. For the final FSM-ACT policy’s ACT model’s training dataset, we only randomised the task board’s position over a radius roughly 33% smaller than the ones the held-out trials actually used, so our ACT models had never learned how to operate the robotic arm when the TCP is much further away. For ACT that is out of the training set’s trajectory distribution, the model had never seen the motor joints for when the TCP is that far from the base and had never been taught how to move when it was, and our APPROACH stage consequently came apart. 

Tragically, nothing downstream can recover from a bad APPROACH, so we lost two out of three held-out evaluation trials before the state machine’s insertion logic ever ran. Between 141 points locally and the 89 points in held-out evals, the gap between these two scores was the part of the scene we never tested in our local benchmarks.

# Lessons

**Lesson 1: Boring wins.** Everything that sounded sexy: RL in Isaac Sim, learned perception in the loop, an end-to-end policy which does the whole task. They either didn’t work or couldn’t ship. What ultimately worked was the unsexy version: collect joint trajectories, imitate them, and make the hard final stage as analytical and control-based as possible. The boring idea, ACT-FSM policy, where we duct-taped together one learned policy and a bunch of maths, wound up getting further than anything we ever tested. 

**Lesson 2: The benchmark is the ceiling.** We can only tell a policy is improving if the benchmark the policy is tested on actually represents the distribution it will be deployed into during inference. Our benchmark didn’t. We built our benchmark on a wrong assumption about the task domain (because we misread the competition docs), we trusted it, and tuned our policies against it. Consequently, we spent our last weeks bench-maxxing a test set that never represented the real-world distribution, and the held-out competition evals burnt us for it.
